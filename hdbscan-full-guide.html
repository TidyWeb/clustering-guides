Density at
every

HDBSCAN: DBSCAN without choosing EPS.

HDBSCAN (Hierarchical DBSCAN) finds crowded areas like DBSCAN does, and leaves isolated points as noise. The difference is that it does not use one fixed EPS. It looks at the data at every EPS, from huge to tiny, and keeps the clusters that last longest. That lets it find a tight town centre and a spread-out housing estate in the same run. Start at the clustering overview, then follow the DBSCAN guide. This HDBSCAN guide continues that route with a new map of pothole reports; after it, continue to K-Means.

01One EPS is
not enough

Crowded is not the same everywhere

In the DBSCAN guide, one EPS (the radius of the circle drawn round each point) decided what counts as crowded, everywhere on the map. That works when every group is about equally crowded. On this new map it is not: the town centre is packed tight, the junction is small and tight, and the housing estate is spread out.

Here is DBSCAN on this map with min_samples = 5 and three different values of EPS. Watch what happens to the estate and to the town centre.

DBSCAN, EPS = 0.5 2 clusters · 24 noise

The town centre and junction are found, but all 20 estate reports are noise.

DBSCAN, EPS = 0.9 3 clusters · 6 noise

The town centre and the junction merge into one cluster, and the estate still breaks into 2 pieces.

DBSCAN, EPS = 1.1 2 clusters · 4 noise

The estate is found, but the town centre and the junction merge into one cluster.

A small EPS misses the spread-out estate. A larger EPS finds the estate but glues the town centre and the junction together. There is no single EPS that gets all three right, because the right EPS is different in different places.

HDBSCAN on the same map min_cluster_size 5

One run, no EPS: 3 clusters, one for each area, and 6 noise reports.

How it differs in plain words

  • K-Means asks: "split the data into K groups". You choose K.
  • DBSCAN asks: "where are points packed closer than EPS?" You choose EPS, and it applies everywhere.
  • HDBSCAN asks: "which crowded areas last longest as EPS changes?" You choose the smallest group you would call a cluster (min_cluster_size). It works out the right level of crowding for each area.
  • Like DBSCAN, it labels isolated points as noise (-1) and allows any shape.

03The big
idea

Try every EPS, keep what lasts

Imagine examining DBSCAN* ("DBSCAN star") at every EPS, from huge to tiny. DBSCAN* groups core points: points with enough nearby neighbours to count as part of a crowd. It treats the other points as noise. Ordinary DBSCAN can also attach a border point—one without enough neighbours itself, but close to a core group. HDBSCAN builds a hierarchy from these changing core groups, then keeps the clusters that survive over the longest range of EPS. Its final result can include points that dropped off earlier, with lower membership strength (Section 09; scikit-learn user guide).

The one setting you mostly choose is min_cluster_size: the smallest number of points you would be willing to call a cluster. Here it is 5. Anything smaller that breaks off is treated as noise.
04Core
distance

How far do you have to reach to find a crowd?

For each report, HDBSCAN finds its 5th nearest report, counting the report itself (this number is min_samples, which by default equals min_cluster_size). The distance to it is the report's core distance. In a busy area the crowd is close, so the core distance is small. In a quiet area you must reach much further.

The core distance is a measure of how crowded each point's own neighbourhood is. It is the same idea as DBSCAN's core point test, turned round: instead of asking "is there a crowd within EPS?", HDBSCAN asks "what is the smallest EPS at which this point would be a core point?" The answer is its core distance.
05Mutual
reachability

A distance that respects crowding

HDBSCAN replaces the plain distance between two points with the mutual reachability distance: the largest of three numbers.

mutual reachability(a, b) = the largest of
  core distance of a,
  core distance of b,
  the plain distance between a and b

In plain words: two points only count as close if they are close to each other and both sit in a crowd. Points in busy areas keep their short distances. Points in quiet areas get pushed apart, so a lone report cannot act as a stepping stone between groups.

Why this matters: DBSCAN's bridge problem came from sparse points linking groups together. Mutual reachability makes sparse links long, so they are the first to be cut in Section 07.
06The tree
of links

Join everything with the shortest links

Using the mutual reachability distances, HDBSCAN joins all 52 reports into one network using exactly 51 links, choosing links so that their total length is as small as possible and there are no loops. This is called a minimum spanning tree. Every report is connected to every other by exactly one path.

Cutting every link longer than some EPS leaves connected groups of core reports. This is the DBSCAN* view: non-core reports are treated as noise. Ordinary DBSCAN can also attach border reports to a nearby core group. HDBSCAN uses these DBSCAN*-style groups to build its tree across EPS values, then selects the groups that persist. The final HDBSCAN result can include reports that fell away later, with lower membership strength (Section 09).
07Lowering
EPS

Cut the longest link, then the next, then the next

Now HDBSCAN lowers EPS from huge to zero. Each time EPS drops below a link's length, that link is cut. Three things can happen: a group splits into two big enough groups (new clusters are born), a small piece breaks off (those reports become noise), or a cluster falls apart entirely.

08Stable
clusters

The cluster tree, and which clusters to keep

The story in Section 07 can be drawn as one picture, the condensed cluster tree. Read it from top to bottom as EPS falls. Each shape is a cluster: it appears at the top, gets thinner as reports drop off, and ends when it splits or fades away.

Stability: how long a cluster holds on to its points

HDBSCAN gives every cluster a stability score. Roughly, it adds up how long each report stays in the cluster as EPS falls. A cluster that appears early and keeps most of its reports for a long time gets a high score. HDBSCAN measures this in "1 ÷ EPS", so time spent at small EPS (very crowded) counts for more.

Choosing: parent or children?

Starting from the bottom of the tree, HDBSCAN compares each cluster with the clusters it splits into. If the parent's stability is at least as large as its children's added together, it keeps the parent; otherwise it keeps the children. This rule is called excess of mass ("eom"), and it is the default. The whole map is never chosen unless you allow a single cluster.

cluster A: its own stability 9.5 vs its children together 75.7 → keep the children.
09The
result

Three clusters, plus how strongly each report belongs

The kept clusters become the labels: cluster 0 is the Hillside estate; cluster 1 is the town centre; cluster 2 is the Mill junction. The 6 reports that fell away before any kept cluster (R03, R06, R19, R35, R42 and R44) get the label -1, noise.

HDBSCAN also gives each report a membership strength between 0 and 1, stored in probabilities_. A report that stays in its cluster down to very small EPS scores close to 1; one that drops off early, near the cluster's edge, scores lower. Noise always scores 0.

Despite its name, probabilities_ is not a true probability. Read it as "how firmly does this point belong here?" It is useful for spotting edge cases worth a second look.

The other way to choose: leaf

Excess of mass "eom", the default

Keeps the most stable clusters, which tends to give fewer, larger clusters.

Leaf cluster_selection_method="leaf"

Keeps only the clusters at the ends of the tree's branches: 3 clusters and 6 noise here. On bigger data, leaf usually gives more, smaller and more even clusters.

label 0: Hillside estatelabel 1: town centrelabel 2: Mill junction×label −1: noise
These two maps match on this example. Both choices assign all 52 reports to the same three clusters or noise; every marker keeps the same label, shape and colour in both maps. That happens because the most stable groups chosen by excess of mass are already the end branches that leaf selection keeps. The rules differ, even though this dataset gives them the same answer.

min_cluster_size default 5

The smallest group you would call a cluster. Too small, and tiny accidental clumps become clusters. Too large, and small real groups, like the 8-report junction here, disappear. On this small map, a value of 12 or more labels every report as noise: a clear sign the setting is too large for the data. Choose it from what a meaningful group means for your question.

min_samples default = min_cluster_size

How many points make a crowd when working out core distances (the same idea as in DBSCAN, and it counts the point itself). Larger values make HDBSCAN more cautious, so more points become noise. Most people leave it at the default at first.

cluster_selection_epsilon default 0

An optional distance below which clusters are not split any further. Useful when you know that differences smaller than, say, 0.5 km do not matter to you.

allow_single_cluster default False

By default HDBSCAN never answers "everything is one cluster". Set it to True if that is a possible real answer for your data.

One difference to watch: scikit-learn's min_samples counts the point itself, but the older stand-alone hdbscan package does not. To get the same results, scikit-learn's value must be one higher (HDBSCAN reference).
11HDBSCAN
in Python

The whole route in pandas and scikit-learn

Four short blocks, using the same reports from town_reports.csv (download it next to your notebook). HDBSCAN is in scikit-learn from version 1.3.

Reading the code. Lines starting with # are comments: notes for people, which Python ignores. All new terms are in the glossary at the end.

Block 1: load, fit and count

# pandas: tables; HDBSCAN: the clustering method
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.cluster import HDBSCAN

# read the reports and keep the two position columns
reports = pd.read_csv("town_reports.csv")
X = reports[["east_km", "north_km"]].to_numpy()

# min_cluster_size: the smallest group we call a cluster
# copy=True: leave our data untouched (also stops a
# warning in scikit-learn 1.8 and 1.9)
# fit = run HDBSCAN on the points. There is no EPS.
hdb = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=5, copy=True).fit(X)

# labels_: one label per report; -1 means noise
labels = hdb.labels_
reports["cluster"] = labels

# count clusters without counting -1
n_clusters = len(set(labels)) - (1 if -1 in labels else 0)
n_noise = (labels == -1).sum()
print(f"clusters: {n_clusters}, noise: {n_noise}")
print(reports.loc[labels == -1, "report"].tolist())

  1. Import pandas and HDBSCAN.
  2. Load the table and keep the numeric position columns as an array.
  3. Fit with just min_cluster_size. min_samples copies it automatically.
  4. Count the clusters and list the noise reports.

Block 2: how strongly does each report belong?

# probabilities_: membership strength, 0 to 1
reports["strength"] = hdb.probabilities_

# per cluster: how many reports, average and weakest
# strength, rounded to 2 decimal places
print(reports.groupby("cluster")["strength"]

Good fit reach for HDBSCAN

  • You do not know how many groups there are, or how crowded they are.
  • Groups are likely to differ in density: city centres and suburbs, busy and quiet products.
  • You want noise points flagged rather than forced into a group.
  • You want a first look at the data with only one setting to think about.

Watch out limits

  • Columns still need sensible units or scaling, exactly as in DBSCAN.
  • With many columns, distances become less meaningful for every method here.
  • min_cluster_size still changes the answer (Section 10). Try a few values.
  • The stability rule can prefer one big cluster over several smaller ones you care about; the leaf method is an alternative.
13Check
yourself

Can you explain it without looking?

1. Why can one EPS fail on this map?

The right EPS for the tight town centre is far too small for the spread-out estate, and the right EPS for the estate is big enough to glue the town centre and junction together.

2. What is a core distance?

The distance from a point to its min_samples-th nearest point, counting itself. Small in crowded areas, large in quiet ones.

3. What does mutual reachability do?

It takes the largest of the two core distances and the plain distance, so points in quiet areas are pushed apart and cannot link groups together.

4. What is the tree of links for?

It holds the answer for every EPS at once: cutting every link longer than EPS leaves the groups DBSCAN would find at that EPS.

5. What happens when a piece smaller than min_cluster_size breaks off?

Its points become noise, and the cluster it left carries on with the rest.

6. What does "stability" measure?

Roughly, how long a cluster holds on to its points as EPS falls. Long-lasting clusters score highly.

7. What is the excess of mass rule?

Keep a parent cluster if its stability is at least that of its children added together; otherwise keep the children.

8. What does probabilities_ tell you?

How firmly each point belongs to its cluster, from 0 to 1. Noise is 0. It is not a true probability.

Transfer challenge. A delivery company clusters drop-off locations for a city and its surrounding villages. DBSCAN either merges the city districts or loses the villages. What would you try, what would you set, and what would you check?
Open a model answer
  • Try HDBSCAN, because city districts and villages have very different densities.
  • Set min_cluster_size to the smallest number of drop-offs that would be worth treating as a group.
  • Check the noise list: are they genuinely isolated addresses?
  • Look at membership strength to find addresses on the edge of a group.
  • Try a few min_cluster_size values, and the leaf method, and see whether the groups stay similar.
  • Make sure the coordinates are in sensible units (for example km, not degrees mixed with metres).
